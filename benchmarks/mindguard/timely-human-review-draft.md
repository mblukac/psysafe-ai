# Time-sensitive human review in AI systems under scarce staffing

**Technical draft · Research question and outcome frozen 2026-11-21**

The [full-cohort MindGuard replay](full-panel-findings.md) and [pending-ticket experiment](ticket-updates.md) report what was actually run. The [loss audit and equal-minute policy comparison](ticket-updates.md#where-cases-are-lost-and-which-fix-buys-the-most-catches) apply the two frozen outcomes below. The customer-support study remains a proposal.

When an AI system flags a conversation, someone still has to be available to read it. A team may have enough reviewer-hours over a week and still miss the cases that arrive together on Tuesday afternoon. We ask: **When a small team faces a surge, which conversations reach a human in time, which slip through, and would changing the order of review actually help?**

We study this question first in mental-health conversations and then in customer support. In one setting a clinician endorses a risk signal; in the other an annotator identifies a point where a bot should hand the conversation to a person. The two settings need not have the same urgency, deadlines, or consequences. What they share is a decision: a reviewer becomes free while several conversations may need attention. Reinforcement learning (RL) is one way of making that decision. Whether it adds anything over a simple rule is an empirical question.

## Frozen framing: who reaches a human, and what can that human see?

The subject is **timely human review under scarce staffing**, not which classifier wins. Reviewing one conversation now can make another miss its deadline. Waiting can reveal a more informative turn, but it spends time that the person waiting cannot get back. These trade-offs differ: a later turn can improve what a reviewer sees without moving anyone forward in the queue.

Start each case's clock at the **first observed evidence of need** according to the reference annotations, never the model's first alert. Ask two questions on the **same denominator of all observed need-positive conversations**, including those never flagged, expired or never served:

1. **Timely access:** did a human review start between that first evidence and the setting-specific deadline?
2. **Timely, evidence-supported review (primary):** at that timely start, was the turn selected for the reviewer independently endorsed? In the replay this is the endpoint of the prefix shown, not a claim that a person read, agreed with or acted on it.

Compare policies on the same arrivals and on-duty staffing, and report the actual reviewer-minutes consumed. Equal seats need not mean equal labor; in the replay, busy minutes come from assumed service durations, not observed reading times. Changing admission or dispatch can change **who** gets served; changing presentation alone at identical starts can change only the second outcome if service times are held fixed. Decision models provide signals for these choices; thresholds, queue rules, Bayesian updates and RL are candidate policies, not the paper's thesis. A mental-health endorsement is not a diagnosis, and a timely review is not evidence of harm prevented or a customer problem resolved.

The [critical reading notes](#critical-reading-notes) establish why this is a measurement and allocation question, not a claim to have invented queue-aware handoff.

## The decision and the outcome

Separate three decisions. **Detection** determines whether and when a conversation generates a ticket. **Dispatch** determines which waiting ticket gets the next free reviewer. **Staffing** determines when that reviewer is available. In the main comparison, freeze the detector and staffing so that dispatch policies see exactly the same tickets. Vary detector sensitivity and staffing in separate comparisons. Otherwise a policy that sends fewer alerts, or one that uses more reviewer-hours, can look like a better scheduler.

The primary outcome is **timely review recall**: among conversations with at least one endorsed need, the fraction for which a human starts reviewing a supported turn within the specified window. Count each conversation at most once. The clock starts **from the first observed evidence of need**: the first majority-endorsed turn for the relevant MindGuard endpoint, or the first annotated handoff opportunity in support. This origin is the same for every model, even one that never flags the case. An unsupported early flag gets no retrospective credit for a later disclosure. This measures access to review, not whether a reviewer resolved the issue or prevented harm.

For the initial comparison, each conversation can generate one ticket, at the first eligible turn crossing a frozen threshold. The ticket holds that prefix. A reviewer who starts it stays occupied until service finishes. The dispatcher sees scores and messages available by decision time, queue age and current staffing; it does not see clinician votes, true onset, future arrivals or future utterances. The service may expire a ticket based on time since the **alert**; that observable rule is distinct from the **onset** deadline used to score it. Updating an existing ticket when new evidence arrives is a separate comparison, not a change made only for RL.

## Evidence for the two applications

**Mental health.** Use all 67 conversations and 1,134 clinician-rated user turns in the [MindGuard expert release](https://arxiv.org/html/2602.00950v2#S3). For a desk covering both risk categories, 17 conversations contain an observed majority-endorsed turn: nine for self-harm (20 turns) and eight for harm to others (22 turns). Report overall access and each category separately. Neither label means depression or distress in general. Preserve all three individual votes for a sensitivity analysis of disagreement.

The [earlier staffing replay](reviewer-surges.md) used only 50 conversations, eight with majority self-harm endorsement. Requiring every position in a conversation to be rated discarded 392 **rated** turns because of 22 **unrated** positions in 17 conversations. Here each entire path enters the workload. Keep the original turn indices and elapsed time; the main replay offers a score at each rated position and makes no claim about a decision at an unscored position. Only one confirmed self-harm case has a gap before its first observed endorsement, exactly one turn earlier. Sixteen other paths could contain self-harm endorsement only in a gap despite having none observed. Bound comparisons using the same possible missing labels for every policy; retain the complete-case analysis as a check. The previous recall estimates are not results for this cohort.

For policy development, the [separate MindGuard training release](https://huggingface.co/datasets/swordhealth/MindGuard-trainingset) offers 644 synthetic conversations, 6,115 labeled user turns and 339 scenarios. It can supply more varied queues than repeating nine self-harm paths. Its judge saw the full conversation; these are development labels, not clinician evidence for when a signal was visible live. Use only the prefix available at each decision. Split by conversation family before resampling, and keep copies on the same side of each split. Freeze policies before scoring held-out families. The expert set has already informed this design; it is an internal benchmark, not untouched external validation.

**Customer support.** [Liu et al., *Time to Transfer*](https://github.com/LauJames/MHCH-DAMI) provide 7,500 real e-commerce bot conversations annotated for turns appropriate for human handoff. Apply the same access metric from the first annotated handoff opportunity, with a support-specific window. The public release provides word IDs and turn features, not reusable raw text or reviewer timestamps: use a predictor compatible with those features. The [ServiceNow incident log](https://doi.org/10.24432/C57S4H) separately records opening times, updates and priorities for 24,918 incidents. Use it to check whether high-priority arrivals cluster in busy periods. It neither links to the chats nor measures active review duration. This is a second test of the allocation method, not a transfer of clinical labels or deadlines into support.

## Replaying steady demand and surges

Resample **whole conversations**, including ordinary ones. Keep their turn order, scores and endorsements together. Assign them to a clock drawn from real arrivals: [BurstGPT](https://arxiv.org/abs/2401.17644) supplies AI request times; [Open Up](https://formative.jmir.org/2024/1/e46823/) supplies crisis-service visitor interarrivals. An AI request is not a conversation or a safety alert. State how many requests are sampled as monitored conversation starts. Timing is observed; the assignment of review need to those times is simulated.

| Comparison | What changes | What stays fixed |
|---|---|---|
| Steady vs bursty arrivals | Evenly space the same starts, then restore their observed times | Number of conversations, paths, horizon and service requirements |
| Steady vs bursty need | Move more need-positive paths into selected windows, swapping whole paths | Exact arrival times, path counts and overall prevalence |
| Combined surge | Concentrate need in the busy windows of the bursty clock | Same underlying case set as the steady control |
| Higher sustained load | Increase monitored arrivals per hour | Case mix, service-time distribution and deadline; do not speed up the reviewers |

Start with one, two and three reviewers **simultaneously on duty**, plus an immediate-service control. For comparability, the mental-health pilot reference is ten minutes to *start*, twenty minutes of service, and two minutes between user turns. These are scenario values, not measured clinical requirements. Vary service time, including variation across tickets, and vary the deadline. Use identical service draws for the same ticket across dispatch policies. Separately compare flat coverage with more shift overlap during *forecast* peaks at equal scheduled staff-hours; record actual busy minutes too. A retrospective peak label may describe where misses occurred, but it cannot guide the simulated live dispatcher.

## What better dispatch could recover

[Adusumilli, Geiecke and Schilter](https://arxiv.org/html/1904.01047v5) frame allocation as a trade-off between acting now and preserving capacity for another arrival. They estimate gains from treatment using outcome data. Here capacity returns when service finishes, tickets can wait, and the measurable reward is a timely supported review. We borrow the allocation principle, not a causal estimate of benefit or a guarantee about RL. [Lykouris and Weng](https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.12237) already distinguish admission and scheduling in congested human–AI systems. Our focus is what happens between the first evidence of need in a conversation and the start of human review.

Consider one free reviewer and two tickets. A has a 95% chance of warranting review and 15 minutes left; B has a 60% chance and two minutes left. Each review occupies ten minutes. Taking A first gives 0.95 expected timely warranted reviews: B expires. Taking B first gives **1.55**, because A can still start ten minutes later. The example assumes known time windows to isolate the decision. In the replay, true onset is hidden. A practical comparator should estimate **what a ticket loses by waiting one more service interval**, not simply sort by its risk score.

Before fitting RL, compute three quantities on each identical stream at a fixed alert rule and deadline:

- **U:** recall if every ticket starts immediately. It measures what the detector makes possible.
- **O(c):** best feasible recall with the specified staffing schedule *c*, allowing a hindsight scheduler to know labels, arrivals and service times. It must use the same tickets, release times, service durations and expiry rules; its allowed schedules must include every live policy's schedule.
- **Rπ(c):** recall achieved by a policy π using only information available at dispatch.

For the fixed-prefix endpoint, a delay cannot turn an unsupported or already-late ticket into a catch. On the same positive-case denominator, the missed fraction therefore separates as:

$$
1-R_\pi(c) = (1-U) + \bigl(U-O(c)\bigr) + \bigl(O(c)-R_\pi(c)\bigr).
$$

The first term is missed or mistimed detection. The second is loss **no ordering could avoid** with those reviewers and tickets. The third is the most that better scheduling *and better information together* could recover. It is not a promised RL gain. With enough free reviewers, all dispatch rules start the same tickets immediately. Conversely, an overloaded queue can have many expirations but few meaningful choices if no reviewer becomes free before the deadlines.

## Proposed pilot and criteria for comparison

After review, first reproduce the all-67 observed-turn workload and calculate the immediate-service and hindsight bounds. Verify the no-contention tie and check the hindsight calculation on small queues with known optima before using it to interpret a policy difference. Then compare first-in first-out, highest calibrated risk, earliest estimated deadline and estimated loss from waiting. Pilot RL as a **dispatch-only** policy against the strongest development-selected simple rule. Give both the same information, tickets and staffing. Set alert sensitivity on development data; repeat the paired comparison at different sensitivities rather than tuning a threshold separately for RL on the evaluation cases.

The main display should show **timely recall against actual reviewer-minutes**, with staffing and demand regime labeled. Beside it, show the three sources of missed reviews. Report peak and off-peak recall, ticket expirations, unsupported starts, and how often a free reviewer actually had to choose between tickets. Keep comparisons paired by conversation assignment and arrival block. Report unique positive conversations and opening families alongside resampled volume; vary families and time blocks separately to distinguish uncertainty about cases from sensitivity to traffic. Repeating a role-play improves our understanding of queues, not the size of the clinical evidence base.

The success criterion is more warranted conversations reaching a human *in time*, at comparable reviewer effort. The proposed pilot tests where capacity actually forces a choice. A finding that ordinary dispatch suffices, that shift coverage matters more, or that the detector missed the case before it could enter the queue would each answer the paper's question.

## Critical reading notes

**Scope and screening (2026-11-21).** This is a targeted, critical narrative review, not a systematic review. Parallel searches covered crisis-chat and peer-support triage, automated customer-service handoff, professional moderation, congested deferral, sequential allocation and process monitoring. We read primary methods, results and appendices; then separately challenged the closest precedents and the interpretation of their endpoints. For each paper we asked: who supplied the reference label, what started and stopped the clock, who never reached review, what human capacity was observed or simulated, and what comparison identifies the claimed effect? The following are the claims we retain, not a leaderboard or a pooled effect estimate.

### What was observed in conversations with humans?

- **An early flag is not a timely review.** [MindGuard v2, §§3.1, 5.1.1 and Table 1](https://arxiv.org/html/2602.00950v2#S5.SS1) already compares first flag with first expert-endorsed turn. Its 8B model flagged at or before that turn in 94% of the 17 positive role-plays. An always-flag-first rule would score 100% on that particular timing column, not on the authors' separate false-positive metrics. Three clinicians rated user turns with preceding context, without seeing the reply to that turn. The labels locate observed evidence, not illness onset, a clinical response deadline, or whether a human reviewed the case. [Liu et al., AAAI 2021, Dataset and Golden Transfer Tolerance](https://doi.org/10.1609/aaai.v35i7.16731) likewise measure predictions against annotated transfer opportunities in 7,500 customer-service dialogues; no human operator participated in those dialogues. Neither dataset observes a pickup.
- **The reference clock is itself a judgment.** [Byun et al., CRADLE-Dialogue v4, §3.2 and Appendix B](https://arxiv.org/html/2606.10380v4) distinguish an early warning from confirmation of a particular crisis. Annotating the warning was especially variable across teams. A majority-endorsed turn is a reproducible study convention, not a precisely known instant when a person became unsafe. Agreement and missing ratings matter even if the replay clock is exact.
- **Faster replies among those answered can coexist with unchanged reach.** [Milne et al., 2019, Tables 3–4 and regressions](https://www.jmir.org/2019/4/e11410/) deployed a priority sidebar on the ReachOut youth forum. From prelaunch to post-training, median time to first moderator reply among answered crisis-labeled posts fell from 143 to 29 minutes; 80.6% versus 81.6% received a reply. Crisis labels in the operational analysis were model-assigned. Staffing changed, and the planned crisis-latency contrast did not meet the multiple-comparison threshold. The before/after difference is not a causal reduction, a pickup time for every post, or a health outcome.
- **Real services record different human events.** [Swaminathan et al., 2023, Fig. 1B and Methods, “Analysis of response times”](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10663535/) report daily medians of 8–11 minutes from message to alert and 0–1 minute from alert to a specialist's classification click. The specialist saw the message and a chart link. This is not time to patient contact; negatives missed by both the system and routine escalation generally went unverified. [Glasner et al., 2026, Methods and phase 2 results](https://formative.jmir.org/2026/1/e106118/) routed completed chatbot interactions to clinician outreach. Of 172 people triggering alerts across both conversational and questionnaire pathways, 73 were reached. An outreach attempt, successful contact and a review start are three different endpoints.
- **Scarcity is observable; a simulated staffing gain is not a field effect.** [Chiang, Law & Yip, 2024, Open Up, retrospective evaluation and Fig. 1](https://formative.jmir.org/2024/1/e46823/) use real crisis-chat arrival and service records. In November 2020, 3,493 of 9,175 requests converted to counseling under the paper's definition; not every unconverted request was a queue abandonment. The alternative staffing mix and its projected increase in conversion were simulated. Waiting times calculated only among people who stayed cannot describe access for everyone who requested help.
- **Customer support supplies a field comparison, not a clinical analogy.** [Wang et al., 2026 working paper, §§3–5, Tables 3–5](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2605.14830) randomized a supervised agentic-AI deployment across 647 Taobao workers. Eligible chats were 16.8% shorter on average, while observed customer ratings fell by 0.412 points on a five-point scale. Each chat already had an assigned worker, and ratings were optional. The deployment was randomized; which cases got an early takeover was not. Faster handling is not evidence of a better handoff.

### Which allocation and presentation ideas already exist?

- **Opportunity cost, not treatment effect.** [Adusumilli, Geiecke & Schilter, §§2, 5](https://arxiv.org/html/1904.01047v5) formalize the value of reserving a scarce resource for future arrivals and illustrate it using randomized job-training data. Their main model allocates treatment on arrival; they also discuss queueing extensions. We borrow the opportunity cost. Without outcomes under alternative interventions we cannot borrow their welfare estimand.
- **Waiting for conversational evidence is not new.** [Paek & Horvitz, NAACL 2004, §4.3 and Table 5](https://www.erichorvitz.com/hlt2004.pdf) combine successive speech-recognition results with an operator queue fitted to call-center data. On held-out calls, the queue-aware rule would transfer fewer eventually successful calls, but also fewer eventual failures. [Horvitz & Paek, 2007, §§3, 5, 7](https://www.erichorvitz.com/Horvitz-Paek-2007_Human_AI_Complementary_Computing_Policies.pdf) explicitly reconsider transfer as the dialogue unfolds and as expected operator wait varies. These are counterfactual routing decisions on logs, not measurements of a response to a newly disclosed safety need.
- **Scarce reviewers and changing priorities are not new either.** [Lykouris & Weng, §§2, 6.3–6.4](https://arxiv.org/html/2402.12237v4) jointly study admission, scheduling and the delayed, selective feedback obtained from human reviews; their comment replay simulates service. [Avadhanula et al., §§4–5](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2211.06516#page=7) went further in production: an internal Meta moderation A/B test reported 13% higher severity-and-reach-weighted integrity value at fixed reviewer capacity (95% CI 0.9–25.2%). The endpoint was neither the proportion of people served on time nor measured reviewer-minutes saved. [Gocmen et al., §§3, 5 and Appendix G.5](https://arxiv.org/html/2505.21331v2#A7.SS5) model the option to defer review as more information arrives; the appendix even updates violation probabilities from simulated user reports. Neither waiting nor Bayesian updating is our novelty.
- **Humans have supervised competing live dialogues.** [Muraki et al., IWSDS 2023, §§4–5](https://www.sap.ist.i.kyoto-u.ac.jp/lab/bib/intl/MUR-IWSDS23.pdf) had one operator supervise multiple guide conversations with access to their history and the unanswered question. The trigger was a question the guide could not answer. The authors separately simulated waiting times; they did not grade the evidence available at pickup against an independently marked escalation need.
- **Even the joint framing has precedents.** [Shoush & Dumas, §§3–4](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2109.02894) replay evolving cases in competition for limited resources; their loan-process objective is net intervention gain, not a review deadline tied to independently annotated evidence. [Cánovas-Segura et al., 2023, Table 5](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jbi.2023.104397) already distinguish when a clinical alert becomes relevant, when it is triggered and notified, and when someone acknowledges it. [Purbasari, 2026, §5](https://www.nordjournals.com/index.php/platforma/article/view/1843) proposes measuring time to customer-service handoff and completeness of the transferred context together. It is a proposed evaluation, not a field demonstration; a packet with all expected fields need not point to the disclosure that made the case actionable.
- **What is shown can change how much work a review takes.** [Calabrese et al., ACL 2024, §§3–5](https://aclanthology.org/2024.acl-short.38.pdf) compared evidence formats with 25 professional moderators. Structured span tags reduced mean decision time by 1.34 seconds (7.4%); generic explanations did not significantly speed decisions, and no accuracy gain was detected. These were static hate-speech posts in a controlled task. The study argues against *assuming* fixed reading times in deployment, not for any particular summary or highlight in clinical handoff.
- **A faster process is not necessarily a better outcome.** In a different domain, [Woznitza et al., LungIMPACT randomized trial, Table 2](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13190311/), readers received AI assistance in both arms. Worklist prioritization shortened median chest X-ray reporting time (47.0 to 34.1 hours), but the trial detected no improvement in time to CT or lung cancer diagnosis. This isolates the difference between speeding up one stage and demonstrating benefit downstream; its timescales do not transfer to chat.

**Claims we do not retain.** We do not claim the first queue-aware handoff, first evolving priority, first evidence-bearing alert, or first evaluation relative to an annotated turn. We also do not substitute accuracy on reviewed cases for recall across all cases, treat a quicker reply as harm prevented, or assume resampled role-plays provide independent evidence about more patients. The contribution is a controlled comparison: on the **same conversation streams and human capacity**, distinguish cases lost before admission, cases that wait too long, and timely starts where the selected evidence does not support the alert. Compare those failures across load and sensitivity, without assuming that RL, more alerts, or a newer ticket is better.
